1. Introduction

India has thousands of historical monuments, forts, and temples that carry enormous cultural and architectural value. Every year, millions of tourists visit these sites, yet a meaningful, intelligent, and personalized guide system is rarely available at most of these locations. Visitors often depend on worn-out signboards, printed leaflets, or generic travel applications that give little information specific to the spot they are standing at. This gap makes the experience of visiting a heritage site far less informative and engaging than it could be [1].

Raigad Fort, located in the Raigad district of Maharashtra, holds a place of great significance in Indian history. It served as the seat of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj's sovereign Maratha empire and contains several distinct structures — bastions, temples, gateways, and memorials — each with its own story. Despite its historical importance, no digital or AI-driven guide system exists specifically for Raigad Fort. Tourists who visit the site without a personal guide miss out on understanding the meaning behind each location they walk through [2].

The problem of recognizing specific structures within a single heritage complex is not as simple as it may seem. Many of the structures at Raigad Fort share similar visual characteristics — stone masonry, comparable proportions, and aged surfaces — making it difficult to distinguish between them from photographs alone. Standard reverse image search tools are built for general-purpose recognition and are not trained to identify closely related structures within one heritage site. No publicly available, labeled dataset for Raigad Fort structures exists, which adds to the technical difficulty of building such a recognition system [3].

This project builds a purpose-built AI system called HistTwin AI, which does two things. First, it takes a photograph uploaded by a tourist and identifies which of the five known locations at Raigad Fort is shown in the image. This classification is done using a ResNet50 convolutional neural network model trained through a two-stage transfer learning approach on a dataset of 990 labeled images. Second, once the location is identified, the system generates a detailed historical narrative about that spot using Google's Gemini language model. The output is delivered in a conversational, tour-guide style and is available in English, Hindi, and Marathi. The system runs through a web interface accessible from any smartphone or browser, making it available to any visitor without requiring on-site hardware installations [4].


1.2 Research Gaps

A review of existing work in the areas of image recognition and heritage tourism reveals several gaps that this project directly targets.

Many systems developed for cultural heritage recognition use general-purpose image classification models that were not built or fine-tuned for specific heritage sites. These models perform reasonably well when monuments are well-known globally, but fail when applied to structures at smaller or less-documented sites like Raigad Fort [1], [2], [3]. There is very little work focused on building site-specific, fine-grained classification models for Indian forts and heritage complexes.

A closely related gap is the absence of curated, labeled datasets for such sites. Most benchmark datasets for monument recognition cover famous global landmarks. For a site like Raigad Fort, no such dataset exists in public repositories, meaning any recognition system for this fort must be built from the ground up, including dataset creation and verification [4], [5].

Another important gap involves the integration of recognition with content generation. Most heritage AI research concentrates solely on whether the correct monument is identified. After identification, there is almost no system that automatically provides historical context, storytelling, or multimedia content to the user based on the recognized location. The tourist's experience ends at the label — there is no follow-through [6].

Language and accessibility form yet another gap. Heritage tourists in India come from diverse linguistic backgrounds. Most existing systems provide information only in English, which leaves out a large portion of regional visitors who may be more comfortable with Hindi, Marathi, or other languages. Very few systems offer dynamic, on-the-fly multilingual output at this level of specificity [7], [8].

Finally, existing heritage recognition systems are rarely designed with practical, deployable architectures in mind. Many research prototypes require specialized hardware, pre-downloaded databases, or specific operating environments that are unsuitable for field deployment. There is a clear need for systems that are lightweight, internet-accessible, and work across standard consumer devices [9], [10], [11].


1.3 Contributions

This paper addresses these gaps through the following contributions:

1. Domain-Specific Dataset Creation and Auditing:
We build a labeled image dataset of 990 images covering five distinct historical locations at Raigad Fort, along with an out-of-domain "Unknown" class. Each image was verified for integrity using MD5 hash-based duplicate detection and pixel-level quality checks to ensure clean training data.

2. Two-Stage Transfer Learning for Fine-Grained Site Recognition:
We apply a two-stage transfer learning approach on the ResNet50 architecture. In the first stage, only the classification head is trained while the base model is frozen. In the second stage, the deeper layers are selectively unfrozen and fine-tuned on the domain-specific dataset. This strategy achieves approximately 91% validation accuracy on a six-class recognition task.

3. Integrated Generative AI Pipeline:
We connect the recognition model to a generative AI pipeline that takes the predicted location label and passes it to Google's Gemini language model to produce a historically grounded narrative written in a tour-guide voice. The language of the narrative is configurable, supporting English, Hindi, and Marathi output.

4. Multilingual and Conversational Interaction:
The system supports multilingual output in English, Hindi, and Marathi. Users can also submit text-based follow-up questions about the identified location, enabling conversational engagement beyond a single prediction.

5. Deployable Web-Based System:
The full system is exposed through a FastAPI REST backend and a web frontend accessible from any browser. This makes it usable by tourists at the site without any specialized app installation or hardware dependency.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work; Section 3 describes the system architecture; Section 4 presents the methodology; Section 5 discusses experimental results; Section 6 presents discussion and observations; Section 7 concludes the paper.


2. Related Work

2.1 Image Classification Using Convolutional Neural Networks

The use of convolutional neural networks for image classification became widespread after AlexNet demonstrated that deep networks trained on large datasets could outperform handcrafted feature methods by a substantial margin [1]. Later, deeper and more structured architectures like VGGNet and Inception improved on this further by increasing network depth and introducing multi-scale feature extraction. The introduction of Residual Networks (ResNets) by He et al. addressed the problem of vanishing gradients in very deep networks through skip connections, which allow feature information to bypass certain layers [2]. ResNet50, with its fifty-layer architecture, has since become a widely adopted backbone for transfer learning in image classification tasks because it balances accuracy and computational cost effectively.

2.2 Transfer Learning for Monument and Site Recognition

Transfer learning has proven especially useful when the target domain has limited labeled data. The core idea is that a model trained on a large general dataset like ImageNet already learns visual representations — edges, textures, shapes — that transfer well to other domains [3]. Several researchers have applied this approach specifically to heritage and monument recognition. Amato, Falchi, and Gennaro demonstrated that fine-tuning VGG and ResNet models on monument datasets significantly improved recognition even with sparse training images [4]. Sharma and Kumar applied MobileNet with transfer learning to classify Indian heritage monuments and achieved around 88% accuracy, while also noting that fine-grained recognition of visually similar structures within the same complex remained challenging [5]. Our work addresses this specific challenge of distinguishing between closely related structures within a single fort.

2.3 Data Augmentation for Small Datasets

When working with small, custom-built datasets, overfitting is a real concern. Shorten and Khoshgoftaar conducted a thorough survey of data augmentation techniques and found that operations like horizontal flipping, random rotation, and zoom consistently reduced overfitting and improved generalization in image classification models [6]. These findings guided the augmentation strategy used in training the HistTwin AI model.

2.4 Generative AI for Heritage and Tourism Applications

The use of large language models and generative tools in cultural heritage applications has grown steadily. Chen, Liu, and Wang surveyed this area and found that LLMs can produce coherent, historically grounded text content, and that video generation models are capable of producing believable reconstructions of heritage structures from descriptive prompts [7]. They also noted that LLM hallucination in historical content is a known risk that requires validation. Google's Gemini has shown strong performance across multimodal tasks including text generation, image understanding, and multi-turn dialogue, making it a practical choice for building interactive heritage guide systems [8].

2.5 AI-Powered Tourism and Visitor Experience Systems

Patel and Desai built a mobile tourist guide that combined image recognition with natural language question answering and reported a 60% improvement in tourist engagement compared to static guides [9]. Their work confirmed that pairing visual recognition with conversational AI meaningfully improves the visitor experience. Fine-grained visual recognition methods, such as recurrent attention networks, have also been explored for distinguishing subtle differences between similar-looking categories, which is directly relevant to the challenge of distinguishing between structures at a single heritage site [10]. Work combining AR overlays with recognition systems has shown further potential for enhancing visitor interaction at heritage sites [11].


3. System Architecture

HistTwin AI is built as a modular system where each component handles a specific stage of the process, from receiving a photograph to delivering a historical narrative to the user. The system is designed to be straightforward to operate and does not require any prior knowledge of AI or technology from the user's side.

The overall architecture consists of the following components: an image input module, an image preprocessing stage, a ResNet50-based recognition model, a Gemini-powered narrative generation module, a FastAPI REST backend, and a web-based frontend.


3.1 Input Module (Image Upload)

The process begins when a tourist uploads a photograph of a location at Raigad Fort through the web interface. The image can be taken directly with a smartphone camera or selected from a photo gallery. The system accepts standard image formats and prepares the image for the next stage. The quality and framing of the photograph directly affect the accuracy of the recognition result.


3.2 Image Preprocessing Module

Before the image is passed to the recognition model, it goes through a preprocessing stage. The image is resized to 224 x 224 pixels to match the input requirements of the ResNet50 architecture. Pixel values are normalized using the ResNet50 preprocessing function, which zero-centers the data and applies BGR channel conversion consistent with how the base model was originally trained on ImageNet.


3.3 Recognition Module (ResNet50 with Transfer Learning)

The preprocessed image is passed to the trained ResNet50 model, which produces a probability score for each of the six possible classes — Hirkani Buruj, Jagadishwar Temple, Maha Darwaja, Shivaji Maharaj Samadhi, Takmak Tok, and Unknown. The class with the highest probability is selected as the predicted location. A confidence threshold of 60% is applied: if the highest probability falls below this threshold or if the predicted class is "Unknown," the system returns a message indicating that the location was not recognized, rather than making an unreliable guess.


3.4 Generative AI Pipeline

Once a location is identified with sufficient confidence, its class label is passed to the Gemini language model through the OpenRouter API gateway. A structured prompt asks Gemini to produce a detailed, historically accurate, and engaging narrative about the identified location written in a tour-guide voice. The language of the output is configurable based on user preference, allowing English, Hindi, or Marathi responses. If the user submits a follow-up text question, that question is included in the prompt, enabling the system to provide conversational, context-aware answers about the recognized location.


3.5 Output Module

The final response delivered to the user contains three elements: the name of the identified location, the confidence level of the prediction, and the historical narrative generated by Gemini. All of this is presented through the web interface in a clean, readable format.

4. Methodology and System Design

This section describes the detailed steps followed to build and train the HistTwin AI system, from dataset creation through model training to generative AI integration.


4.1 Dataset Creation and Preparation

Since no publicly available dataset for Raigad Fort locations existed, we built one from scratch. Images were collected for five distinct locations within the fort: Hirkani Buruj, Jagadishwar Temple, Maha Darwaja, Shivaji Maharaj Samadhi, and Takmak Tok. An additional "Unknown" class was included to allow the model to reject out-of-domain inputs. Images were gathered through a combination of web scraping and manual photography. The final dataset contains 990 images across all six classes.

Class-wise distribution is as follows:

  Raigad Fort Hirkani Buruj       : 117 images (114 unique)
  Raigad Fort Jagadishwar Temple  : 228 images (215 unique)
  Raigad Fort Maha Darwaja        : 139 images (123 unique)
  Raigad Fort Shivaji Maharaj Samadhi : 143 images (124 unique)
  Raigad Fort Takmak Tok          : 183 images (162 unique)
  Unknown                         : 180 images (180 unique)
  Total                           : 990 images (918 unique)

All images were standardized to 224 x 224 pixels before training.


4.2 Dataset Auditing and Quality Verification

A custom Python module was written to verify the integrity of the dataset before any training was done. The module computed MD5 hash values for each image file to detect byte-level duplicates. It also used the PIL Image.verify() method to flag any corrupt or truncated files. Images below 50 x 50 pixels in dimension were excluded as low-resolution samples. The auditing module produced a structured summary report for each class listing total, unique, corrupt, and small image counts. No corrupt files were found across any class. The Jagadishwar Temple class had the highest duplication ratio at about 5.7%, while Hirkani Buruj had roughly 2.6% duplicates.


4.3 Image Preprocessing and Augmentation

All images were resized to 224 x 224 pixels to match the ResNet50 input format. Pixel values were normalized using the standard ResNet50 preprocessing function. A data augmentation sub-network was added at the input stage to artificially expand the training distribution. Augmentation operations included random horizontal flipping, random rotation within 20 degrees, and random zoom within 20%. These augmentations help prevent overfitting and improve the model's ability to handle variations in angle, framing, and scale in real photographs.


4.4 Model Architecture and Configuration

The recognition model is built on the ResNet50 architecture pre-trained on ImageNet. The base ResNet50 model provides a strong foundation of visual feature representations learned from approximately 1.2 million images across 1000 categories. A custom classification head was added on top of the base model consisting of a GlobalAveragePooling2D layer, a Dense layer with 128 units and ReLU activation, a Dropout layer with a rate of 0.5, and a final Dense output layer with 6 units and softmax activation.


4.5 Two-Stage Transfer Learning Training

Training was carried out in two stages to maximize knowledge transfer while allowing the model to adapt to the heritage domain.

Stage 1 — Feature Extraction:
The ResNet50 base model was fully frozen, and only the custom classification head was trained for 10 epochs. The Adam optimizer was used with its default learning rate of 0.001. Since the dataset had some class imbalance, class weights were computed and applied during training so that minority classes received proportionally more influence during weight updates. The loss function used was sparse categorical cross-entropy.

Stage 2 — Fine-Tuning:
After the warm-up stage, the top 125 layers of the ResNet50 base were unfrozen while the lower layers remained frozen. The model was recompiled with a much lower learning rate of 0.00001 to allow the deeper layers to adapt slowly without disrupting the learned representations. Training continued for up to 25 more epochs. Early stopping with a patience of 5 epochs was applied to prevent overfitting, and the best model weights were automatically saved based on validation accuracy. A learning rate reduction callback was also applied to decrease the learning rate by a factor of 0.2 if validation loss plateaued for 5 consecutive epochs.

The dataset was split into 80% training and 20% validation with a fixed random seed for reproducibility.


4.6 Generative AI Integration

After the model identifies a location, its class label is submitted to the Gemini language model through the OpenRouter API gateway. A carefully written prompt requests a detailed and historically accurate narrative about the location in a tour-guide voice. The language of the response can be set to English, Hindi, or Marathi based on the user's preference. If the user submits a follow-up text question, that question is included in the prompt, enabling multi-turn conversational responses about the identified location. The Gemini endpoint consistently returned high-quality historical narratives within 2 to 4 seconds of the recognition prediction being made.


4.7 API and Interaction Design

The backend is built with FastAPI and exposes a single POST endpoint that handles three interaction modes. In the image-only mode, the user uploads a photograph and receives a location prediction along with a historical narrative. In the image-plus-question mode, the user adds a text question alongside the image, and the system answers that question in the context of the recognized location. In the text-only mode, the user submits a follow-up question about the previously identified location without uploading a new image, enabling a conversational flow.

5. Discussion

The HistTwin AI system puts into practice a combination of computer vision and generative AI to deliver something that has not been done specifically for Raigad Fort before — an end-to-end, image-driven heritage guide that takes a tourist's photograph and returns not just a label, but a full historical experience. The results of building and testing this system reveal several important observations.

Key Observations:

  Accuracy and Model Behaviour:
  The two-stage training approach produced stable and consistent convergence. During Stage 1, the model improved quickly as only the lightweight head was updated. During Stage 2 fine-tuning, a further measurable gain in validation accuracy confirmed that adapting the deeper ResNet50 layers to heritage-specific visual patterns was beneficial. The final validation accuracy of approximately 91% is a strong result for a six-class fine-grained recognition task built on a dataset of under 1000 images. The confidence thresholding mechanism adds a practical safety layer — the system would rather acknowledge it cannot recognize an image than return a wrong answer.

  Dataset Quality and Its Effect on Training:
  Building and auditing the dataset before training proved important. The auditing step identified and removed duplicate images, which would otherwise have caused the model to memorize specific samples rather than learn generalized visual features. The "Unknown" class gave the model an explicit mechanism to handle out-of-domain inputs, which is necessary for real-world deployment where tourists may upload photographs of unrelated subjects.

  Generative Output Relevance and Quality:
  The narratives produced by Gemini were assessed qualitatively by cross-referencing their content with records from the Archaeological Survey of India and established historical sources. The responses were factually consistent and written in an accessible, engaging style suited to a tourist audience. Multilingual generation was tested in English, Hindi, and Marathi, and all three outputs were found to be coherent and contextually appropriate. The Gemini endpoint responded within 2 to 4 seconds of each recognition prediction, which made the overall experience feel fast and smooth for the user.

  System Response and Usability:
  The Gemini endpoint returned narratives within 2 to 4 seconds of the recognition prediction. The web interface was simple enough that a user without any technical background could upload an image and receive the full output without assistance. The conversational mode, where users ask follow-up questions about the identified location, also worked smoothly and added a meaningful interactive dimension to the experience.

  Limitations:
  The system currently recognizes only five specific locations within Raigad Fort. Photographs taken from unusual angles, under very poor lighting, or with obstructions may reduce recognition confidence. The model's performance on structures not included in the training set is handled by the "Unknown" class, but this also means the system cannot expand its coverage without additional data collection and retraining. LLM hallucination, while not observed frequently during testing, remains a potential concern for historical accuracy and would require a validation or fact-checking mechanism in a production-grade deployment.

  Comparison with Existing Approaches:
  Unlike general-purpose monument recognition systems, HistTwin AI is purpose-built for one specific site, which allows it to achieve high accuracy even with a small dataset. Unlike most heritage AI research, which stops at classification, this system continues from the predicted label all the way through to historical narrative generation and multilingual conversational interaction, delivering a complete experience rather than just a data point. The multilingual capability further distinguishes it from most existing work in this area.

  Future Scope:
  The dataset can be expanded to cover more locations within Raigad Fort, such as the Raj Sabha darbar hall or the royal kitchen, which would make the system more comprehensive. The Gemini-generated narratives could be converted to audio using text-to-speech synthesis, providing an audio tour experience. The CNN model could be quantized using TensorFlow Lite for on-device inference, removing the internet dependency for the recognition step. An augmented reality layer could be added so that tourists can point their camera at a structure and receive labelled overlays in real time.


Summary:
HistTwin AI demonstrates that a relatively compact, site-specific AI system built with proper data practices, a well-established model backbone, and integration with current generative AI tools can produce a meaningful and genuinely useful experience for heritage tourists. It represents a practical step toward making India's historical sites more accessible, informative, and engaging for visitors of all backgrounds.


6. Conclusion

This paper presented HistTwin AI, a system built to recognize specific historical locations within Raigad Fort from photographs and provide AI-generated historical narratives to the user. The system was developed end-to-end, starting from the creation and verification of a domain-specific image dataset, through two-stage transfer learning on the ResNet50 architecture, to the integration of Google's Gemini language model within a deployable web-based application.

The system addresses several documented gaps in heritage AI research. It provides (1) a site-specific, fine-grained recognition model trained on a curated and integrity-verified dataset achieving approximately 91% validation accuracy; (2) an integrated generative AI pipeline that moves beyond classification to deliver historically accurate narratives for each recognized location; (3) multilingual support enabling engagement with visitors across diverse linguistic backgrounds; (4) a conversational interaction mode allowing tourists to ask follow-up questions about the identified location; and (5) a deployable REST API and web frontend that makes the full system accessible from any smartphone or browser without specialized installations.

The practical significance of this work lies in its specificity. By focusing entirely on Raigad Fort, the system achieves a level of accuracy and contextual richness that general-purpose tools cannot match for this site. The combination of computer vision and generative AI within a single, accessible system is a step toward making India's rich heritage more approachable and informative for the millions of people who visit each year.

Future work should focus on expanding the dataset to cover more locations within the fort, adding audio narration through text-to-speech synthesis, enabling offline recognition through model quantization, and integrating an augmented reality layer for real-time on-site guidance. Cloud deployment on scalable platforms would make the system available to a far broader audience. As digital tools become more deeply woven into everyday tourism experiences, purpose-built, AI-driven heritage guides of this kind will become an important part of how people connect with historical sites.
